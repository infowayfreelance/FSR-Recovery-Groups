# Car Stuck in Snow on a Country Lane? What to Do, and When to Call for a Winch-Out

If your car is stuck in snow, take your foot off the accelerator. Spinning the wheels digs the car deeper and polishes the snow under the tyre into ice. Put your hazard lights on and check you are not in anyone's path. Then clear the snow from around the driven wheels, put something gritty or rubbery in front of them, and pull away gently in the highest sensible gear. Give it a few careful tries. If the car won't move, is sliding towards a ditch or verge, is on a slope, or is blocking the lane, stop trying and call for help. A [winch-out service](/services/winch-out) is often the safer choice.

This guide is written for the lanes, farm tracks and hill roads of the North East, where one wrong move can put a car in a wall or a ditch. It is for being **stuck**, not broken down. If your car has failed on a main road, our guide to [breaking down on the A66 in winter](/blog/broken-down-on-the-a66-in-winter) is the better read.

## First things first: make yourself safe

The first 60 seconds matter more than the digging. Before you get out, run through this:

1. **Stop the wheels spinning.** Ease off the accelerator and let the car settle.
2. **Switch your hazard lights on.** Snow muffles sound and cuts visibility, so other drivers may not see you until late.
3. **Look at where you are.** Are you on a bend, at the brow of a hill, or across the road? A car stuck in a blind spot is a danger to you and to others.
4. **Look at the ground.** Is there a steep drop, a ditch or a water-filled verge beside you? If the car could slide that way, don't try to drive it out.
5. **Check your exhaust.** If snow has built up around the tailpipe, don't run the engine until it is clear. Fumes can leak into the cabin.

If you get out, put on a hi-vis jacket if you have one, and keep well away from moving traffic. On a quiet lane the car itself is often the safest thing to stay near. Don't wander off in poor weather to look for signal or help unless you have to.

## How to free a car stuck in snow: step by step

Stay calm and work through these in order.

1. **Straighten the steering wheel.** Turned wheels plough snow in front of them. Straight wheels roll more easily.
2. **Turn off anything that drains the engine.** Switch off heated seats and the blower if you can. You need the car to be as simple as possible to move.
3. **Clear the snow around the driven wheels.** Which wheels drive the car depends on the vehicle, so check your handbook if you're unsure. Dig out the snow in front of and behind those tyres. Clear the gap under the car too. If snow is packed against the underside, the car may be resting on it with the wheels barely touching the ground.
4. **Make a path.** Clear a short, gentle ramp in the direction you want to move, a car's length or so.
5. **Add grip.** Put something rough or rubbery in front of each driven tyre: grit, sand, a bag of cat litter, or your rubber car mats. Push the mats in firmly, ribbed side up, so the tyre can bite and roll over them.
6. **Pull away gently.** See below.
7. **Don't give up the ground you've gained.** If the car moves a short way and stops, stop too. Clear again and add more grip before the next try.
8. **Keep going in a straight line.** Once the car moves, keep it rolling gently until you reach firmer, cleared ground. Don't stop on a slope if you can avoid it.

### Pulling away

Most modern cars do best with gentle, smooth power. Use second gear in a manual if your handbook allows it. In an automatic, select drive and follow any winter or snow mode in the handbook. Bring the clutch up slowly, or squeeze the accelerator the way you would on a wet road. If the wheels start to spin, ease off straight away. Sudden power breaks the grip you've just made.

### Rocking the car, gently

If the car won't move, you can try rocking it out. Move forward a short way, release, let the car roll back, then move forward again with a little more momentum. Keep each movement small and smooth. This works best when the car is only a few inches stuck. If the wheels are spinning without moving the car at all after a few rocks, stop. Spinning only makes the hole deeper.

## Ice, hills and lay-bys: what changes

**Ice.** Snow often sits on top of ice, especially on shaded stretches and at the edge of the road. On ice, tyres make almost no noise, a point the [Highway Code makes in rule 231](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/the-highway-code/driving-in-adverse-weather-conditions-226-to-237). If you're stuck on ice, grit or sand under the tyres matters more than digging. Be careful when you stand on the surface to put it down. Use a gentle touch on the pedals and don't hurry.

**Hills.** Being stuck on a slope is the situation most likely to turn from awkward into dangerous. A car can slide backwards or sideways as soon as it loses grip. If you are on a climb on a road such as the [A68](/areas/a68), put the handbrake on, hazards on, and think carefully before you try to move. Do not attempt to reverse down a steep, icy hill. If the car won't climb on the second or third try, call for help instead of continuing.

**Lay-bys and driveways.** These often collect snow that isn't cleared by gritters. The surface can hide ice, soft verge or a kerb. Clear around the wheels as usual, but check what is under the snow before you drive across it. If the car is stuck half on a verge, don't try to drive it straight out sideways.

## What not to do when you're stuck

A few well-meant fixes can make things worse, or put you at risk.

- **Don't keep revving.** Spinning wheels melt and polish the snow into ice and dig the car in further. The Highway Code (rule 231) advises slow, gentle driving in as high a gear as possible, and this applies when you're pulling away too.
- **Don't try a rope or strap tow.** A DIY tow with a passing vehicle can snap, shift suddenly or damage both cars. If you need pulling out, that is a job for a recovery operator with the right equipment.
- **Don't pour boiling water on the ice.** It can crack glass, and it will refreeze into a worse surface within minutes.
- **Don't stand behind a wheel while someone else drives.** Snow, grit and mats can fire out at speed.
- **Don't run the engine with the exhaust blocked.** Check that the tailpipe is clear before running the engine for heat.
- **Don't push on if the car is moving the wrong way.** If it is sliding towards a drop, the verge or oncoming traffic, stop and call for help.

## When to stop trying and call for help

Digging yourself out is a good first move. It stops being a good one when the risk goes up or the effort stops working. Call for help when:

- the car is **sliding or drifting** towards a ditch, verge, wall or drop
- you are on a **slope** and the car won't hold its position
- the car is **blocking the lane** or sits somewhere other drivers can't see it
- the car is **partly off the road surface**, on soft ground or in a ditch
- you've made **a few careful attempts** and the car hasn't moved
- you are **cold, tired or alone**, and the effort is making things worse
- the wheels have **dug in** to the point where the underside is resting on snow

If the car is a danger to other road users, and you can't make it safe, you should also tell the police. Our [roadside assistance](/services/roadside-assistance) team can help once you're safe and the car is where it is.

## What happens during a winch-out

A winch-out is a controlled way of pulling a stuck car back onto firm ground, using a cable fixed to a recovery vehicle. FSR Recovery Group's winch-out service covers cars and suitable light vans stuck in mud, wet grass, soft ground, loss of traction and awkward roadside positions, and the service is listed as 24/7.

Here is what to expect:

1. **You call and describe the situation.** The more detail you give, the better.
2. **The operator assesses the location and the ground.** They look at where the car is, which way it faces, how it's positioned and what's around it, before any equipment is attached.
3. **The car is winched in a controlled way, where conditions allow.** The operator works slowly and keeps people well clear of the line.
4. **If the car can't continue, recovery is the next step.** If the car is damaged or not safe to drive after it's freed, [vehicle breakdown recovery](/services/vehicle-breakdown-recovery) can take it from there.

**What to tell the operator when you call:**
- your location: road name or number, nearest village or landmark, and direction of travel
- the make and type of vehicle, and whether it's a car or a light van
- how the car is sitting: on the road, on a verge, on a slope, facing a ditch
- whether it's blocking the lane
- whether anyone is inside, and whether anyone is hurt

Stay clear of the winch line while it's under load. Keep everyone back and let the operator do the work.

## Winter kit for North East country lanes

[Highway Code rule 228](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/the-highway-code/driving-in-adverse-weather-conditions-226-to-237) suggests carrying an emergency kit in winter, including a shovel, torch, warm clothing and boots, a first aid kit and some food and drink. For Teesdale, Weardale and the hill roads around [Barnard Castle](/areas/barnard-castle), it's worth adding:

- a **small folding shovel**
- **grit, sand or cat litter** in a bag
- a pair of **rubber mats** (your car's own work well)
- an **ice scraper and de-icer**
- a **fully charged phone and a car charger**
- **gloves, a hat and a blanket**
- a **hi-vis jacket and a torch**
- a **flask** and some food

Cold weather also tests your battery. If the engine is slow to start on a freezing morning, our guide to a [flat battery on a cold morning](/blog/flat-battery-cold-morning) explains what to check. If you're driving across moorland such as the [A66](/areas/a66), tell someone your route and expected arrival time.

Before you set off, check the [Met Office weather warnings](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/weather/warnings-and-advice/uk-warnings). If a warning for snow or ice is in force, rule 228 says not to drive unless your journey is essential.

## After you're moving again: quick checks

Once the car is free, pull up somewhere safe on firm ground before you drive off. Then:

1. **Walk round the car.** Look at the tyres, bumpers and wheel arches. Clear any packed snow and ice from the wheel arches so it doesn't block the wheels.
2. **Look underneath.** Check for anything hanging, dragging or damaged.
3. **Check the dashboard.** If any warning light comes on, or the car feels or sounds wrong, stop and get it looked at.
4. **Drive gently.** Brake and steer smoothly, leave a big gap, and stay in a high gear. Rule 230 warns that stopping distances in snow and ice can be up to ten times greater than on a dry road.
5. **Rethink the route.** If the lane that caught you out is the only way home, consider another road, or wait until it has been treated.

## Frequently asked questions

**What should you do if your car gets stuck in snow?**
Stop spinning the wheels, switch on your hazard lights, and check you're in a safe place. Clear the snow around the driven wheels, add grit or a rubber mat in front of them, and pull away gently. If the car doesn't move after a few careful tries, or it's on a slope or near a drop, call for help.

**Should traction control be on or off in snow?**
It depends on the car, so follow your handbook. Some guides say to switch it off to allow a little wheel spin, while others advise leaving it on. Your manufacturer's advice for your model is the one to trust.

**Is it safe to stay in the car if you're stuck in snow?**
Usually, yes, if you're in a safe position and not in the path of traffic. Keep the exhaust clear of snow before running the engine, keep your hazards on, and don't leave the engine running for long periods. If the car is a danger to others, get out and stand well clear.

**Can you use cat litter or floor mats to get unstuck?**
Yes. Both work well. Put cat litter, grit or sand in front of the driven tyres, or push rubber mats firmly under them. Mats can be thrown out or marked by the tyre, so use old ones if you can.

**When should I call a winch-out service instead of trying again?**
Call when the car is sliding towards a ditch or verge, is on a slope, is blocking a lane, is partly off the road, or hasn't moved after a few careful attempts. Our [winch-out service](/services/winch-out) is there for exactly these situations.

**Will spinning the wheels damage my car?**
It can add wear to the tyres and drivetrain, and it usually makes the car more stuck. Gentle attempts are fine. Repeated hard spinning is best avoided. If you're unsure whether something has been damaged, have it checked.
